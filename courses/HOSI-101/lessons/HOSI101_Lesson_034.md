## 2. Why this matters

Being good at a practiced task does not guarantee equal performance in a different task, setting, or format. This lesson teaches a systems-first explanation so that learners can separate mechanism, context, and uncertainty from identity or blame.

## 3. Learning objectives

- Distinguish near transfer from far transfer.
- Evaluate broad claims that training one task upgrades unrelated abilities.
- Design practice that varies context while preserving core principles.

## 4. Vocabulary

transfer; near transfer; far transfer; generalization; context; similarity

## 5. Normal human-system baseline

Being good at a practiced task does not guarantee equal performance in a different task, setting, or format.

## 6. Core science

[B] Transfer describes using learning in a new context. Near transfer involves relatively similar tasks; far transfer involves more distant skills or settings.

[B] Retrieval-practice research shows that test-enhanced learning can transfer under some conditions, but the size and reliability depend on the relationship between practice and later task. [S13]

[A/B] Broad “brain training” claims deserve special skepticism. Meta-analytic reviews of working-memory training find improvements on trained or similar tasks but little convincing evidence for broad gains in intelligence or unrelated academic skills under rigorous controls. [S14]

[C] Far transfer is not impossible in every domain; results depend on intervention, outcome, population, and study quality. The correct evidence stance is conditional, not absolute.

## 7. Historical context

The field moved from simple “storage box” metaphors toward interacting cognitive systems, computational models, neuropsychology, and network-level neuroscience. Historical models remain useful when treated as models rather than literal maps of a single person's brain.

## 8. Evidence map

- B — Transfer can occur, especially when tasks share relevant processes. [S13]
- A/B — Working-memory training does not reliably produce broad far-transfer benefits in rigorous meta-analysis. [S14]

HOSI ratings: **A = high confidence; B = moderate confidence; C = limited/mixed; D = preliminary/emerging; E = insufficient.** These are educational labels, not regulatory grades.

## 9. What is known

The strongest evidence supports conditional transfer rather than a general rule that training one task upgrades unrelated abilities.

## 10. What is uncertain

The mechanisms that make some learning generalize remain an active research area.

## 11. Condition-specific applications

Do not promise that a generic cognitive game will treat ADHD, PTSD, bipolar disorder, OCD, or TBI through “brain strengthening.”

## 12. Combination/comorbidity panel

Generalization should be tested directly in the real-life task that matters, especially when multiple conditions affect function.

## 13. Lived-experience perspective

A person may describe the same cognitive phenomenon in different language: “I can do it here but not there,” “I know the rule but freeze in the real situation,” or “practice didn't carry over.” HOSI treats these reports as valuable observations while keeping them distinct from diagnostic conclusions and population-level evidence.

## 14. Practical skill / exercise

Choose a skill you learned in one context. Design three increasingly different practice contexts and predict where transfer may fail.

## 20. Homework

## 21. Instructor answer key / rubric

Require conditional reasoning; reject both “transfer always works” and “transfer never exists.” Full-credit work connects claims to the actual outcome and distance from the trained task.

Grade for conceptual accuracy, evidence calibration, reasoning, application, clarity, and scope/ethics. Do **not** grade a learner's personal beliefs, diagnosis, treatment choices, or willingness to disclose lived experience.
