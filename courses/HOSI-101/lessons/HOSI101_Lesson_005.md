## 2. Why this matters

Complex human problems invite simple stories. Sometimes simple stories are correct. Often they are merely satisfying.

Systems thinking provides tools for asking how components interact over time, how feedback can amplify or stabilize change, where delays occur, and why an intervention that helps one part of a system can create an unintended effect somewhere else.

## 3. Learning objectives

- identify components, relationships, feedback loops, delays, and leverage points;
- distinguish reinforcing from balancing feedback;
- explain why a systems diagram is a model rather than proof;
- separate correlation, hypothesized causality, and demonstrated causal evidence;
- build a small causal-loop map while recording uncertainty.

## 4. Vocabulary

system; boundary; feedback loop; reinforcing loop; balancing loop; delay; leverage point; emergence; path dependence; causal-loop diagram

## 5. Normal human-system baseline

Human behavior changes over time and in context. A poor night of sleep might influence attention the next day; poor performance could increase stress; stress could then make sleep harder. That story is plausible, but every arrow needs appropriate evidence before it is treated as established causation.

## 6. Core science

[B] Systems-thinking frameworks emphasize interconnections, feedback, leverage points, dynamic behavior, mental models, and, where appropriate, simulation. [F07]

[B] Causal-loop diagrams can help represent reinforcing and balancing loops and expose missing knowledge, but they are models constructed from assumptions and evidence. They should be iteratively refined as evidence changes. [F08]

[B] Complex health systems research demonstrates that relationships among components can matter in ways missed by static component lists. [F07][F08]

## 7. Historical context

Reductionism asks what the parts are and how each part works. Systems approaches ask how parts interact and what patterns emerge at the level of the whole. These are not enemies. Useful science often needs both.

A systems approach becomes unhelpful when it is so broad that every variable is connected to everything and no claim can be tested.

## 8. Evidence map

- **B —** Systems thinking is useful for analyzing interconnections and dynamic feedback in complex health-related systems. [F07][F08]
- **B/C —** Specific causal-loop models vary in quality because their arrows depend on evidence, assumptions, stakeholder input, and boundary choices.

## 9. What is known

Feedback exists when a change influences processes that eventually feed back into the original variable. Reinforcing loops amplify direction of change; balancing loops oppose change around some state or goal.

## 10. What remains uncertain

Real human systems may include nonlinear effects, thresholds, delays, unmeasured variables, and relationships that differ by person or setting. A classroom map cannot reveal all of them.

## 11. Condition-specific applications

In condition modules, systems thinking can help learners separate mechanisms. For example, avoidance could be influenced by threat perception, immediate relief, social context, fatigue, learning history, or environmental reinforcement. The diagram organizes candidate processes; it does not diagnose which one is operating in a specific individual.

## 12. Combination/comorbidity lens

Comorbidity increases the number of plausible interactions. The disciplined response is not to draw more arrows for dramatic effect. It is to label each link by evidence status and prioritize explanations that can actually be investigated.

## 13. Lived-experience perspective

People often recognize feedback loops in their own lives before research has measured the exact pathway: “When this happens, that gets harder, then the first thing gets worse.” Such observations can generate useful hypotheses. Personal patterns should remain personal evidence until broader evidence supports generalization.

## 14. Practical skill / exercise

Draw this fictional system:

- Deadline pressure
- Sleep opportunity
- Fatigue
- Attention
- Task progress
- Perceived stress

Add arrows, then mark each as one of:
- **E** = supported by evidence you can cite;
- **O** = direct observation in the fictional scenario;
- **H** = hypothesis;
- **?** = uncertain.

Identify one reinforcing loop and one possible balancing intervention.

## 15. Safety / scope

Do not use systems diagrams to infer a diagnosis, hidden trauma, intentionality, or treatment need. A diagram can assist reasoning but cannot replace clinical evaluation where clinical care is relevant.

## 17. Reflection

Think of a non-sensitive routine that has a self-reinforcing pattern. What evidence distinguishes your observation from a causal explanation?

## 18. Discussion prompt

When does adding more variables improve a model, and when does it simply make the model impossible to test?

## 19. Knowledge check / quiz

1. What is the difference between a reinforcing and balancing loop?
2. Why must system boundaries be explicit?
3. Does a causal-loop arrow prove causality?
4. What is a delay?
5. What makes a leverage point a hypothesis rather than a guarantee?

## 20. Homework

Build a causal-loop diagram for a neutral learning or workflow problem. Include 6–10 variables, at least one loop, one delay, one possible leverage point, and evidence-status labels on every arrow.

## 21. Scholar challenge

Find a published causal-loop diagram in health or public policy. Identify at least two relationships that are evidence-backed and two that appear more model-dependent or assumption-dependent.

## 22. Instructor answer key / rubric

Grade for correct loop logic, explicit boundaries, evidence-status labeling, and refusal to equate a modeled arrow with proven causality. Complexity alone earns no points. Humans have enough decorative arrows already.
