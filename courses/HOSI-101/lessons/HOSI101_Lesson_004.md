## 1. Original visual / cover

Visual brief: interlocking translucent layers labeled brain/body, attention, learning, emotion, motivation, sleep, behavior, relationships, environment, development, and health. A prominent footer states: “Metaphor, not literal computer architecture.”

Accessible alt text: layered systems diagram illustrating interacting human processes while clearly noting that the operating-system comparison is metaphorical.

## 2. Why this matters

The phrase “human operating system” is memorable, which makes it useful and dangerous. Useful metaphors simplify complex ideas. Bad metaphors quietly become fake biology.

HOSI uses the term to remind learners to ask about interacting systems before jumping to one label. It does **not** claim that the brain is software, that behavior follows deterministic code, or that people can be debugged like machines.

## 3. Learning objectives

- explain the operating-system metaphor and its limits;
- identify major system layers relevant to human functioning;
- distinguish a systems model from literal anatomy;
- explain feedback, context, and emergence in plain language;
- use a systems map without treating it as proof.

## 4. Vocabulary

system; component; interaction; feedback; context; emergence; leverage point; model; metaphor; dynamic system

## 5. Normal human-system baseline

Ordinary performance depends on many interacting processes. Studying, for example, can involve sensory input, attention, working memory, prior knowledge, motivation, fatigue, emotion, sleep, environmental interruption, physical health, and social expectations.

WHO’s life-course brain-health framework similarly emphasizes multiple interacting determinants rather than one isolated cause. [F02][F03]

## 6. Core science

[B] Systems thinking emphasizes interconnections, feedback, dynamic behavior, multiple perspectives, and leverage points. In health applications, causal-loop and systems models are used to represent hypotheses about how components interact. [F07][F08]

[B] Human brain health spans cognitive, sensory, social-emotional, behavioral, and motor domains and is influenced by physical health, environments, learning, social connection, safety, and access to services. [F02][F03]

The HOSI operating-system metaphor therefore represents **levels of interaction**, not computer parts.

## 7. Historical context

Science often advances through models: atoms as billiard balls, memory as storage, brains as computers, genes as blueprints. Models can organize thinking while also importing misleading assumptions.

Computer metaphors helped cognitive science articulate information processing, but living nervous systems develop, metabolize, adapt, repair imperfectly, interact with bodies and environments, and do not possess a single central programmer.

## 8. Evidence map

- **B —** Systems methods are useful for representing interconnection, feedback, and dynamic behavior. [F07][F08]
- **B —** Brain health/functioning is multidomain and context-sensitive. [F02][F03]
- **Model label —** “Human operating system” is a pedagogical metaphor rather than a scientifically established anatomical entity.

## 9. What is known

Human outcomes often emerge from interactions among multiple variables. Changing one component can alter another component, and effects can differ by context.

## 10. What remains uncertain

The boundaries among cognitive, emotional, social, and biological “systems” are partly analytical conveniences. Different scientific fields divide the same phenomena differently.

## 11. Condition-specific applications

A condition-specific lesson may map which ordinary systems are commonly implicated, but the map cannot establish that every person with the diagnosis has the same mechanism.

For example, difficulty concentrating could plausibly involve sleep, stress, attention regulation, medication effects, pain, mood, environment, injury, or combinations. The correct explanation depends on evidence about the individual context, not on a pretty diagram.

## 12. Combination/comorbidity lens

Combinations are where systems thinking becomes particularly useful. Instead of asking “Which diagnosis owns this symptom?” ask:
- when does it happen?
- what precedes it?
- what follows it?
- what changes it?
- which systems could plausibly interact?
- which explanations have evidence?

## 13. Lived-experience perspective

People may experience their internal processes as tightly connected even when scientific measurement treats them separately. “When I sleep badly, everything becomes harder” is meaningful lived information. Determining which mechanisms explain that experience is a separate scientific question.

## 14. Practical skill / exercise

Choose a neutral activity such as getting ready for class. Draw nodes for:
- sleep/energy
- attention
- memory
- motivation
- emotion
- environment
- physical state
- social context

Add arrows only when you can explain what you think the arrow means. Mark each arrow as:
- directly observed by you;
- supported by a cited source;
- plausible hypothesis;
- unknown.

## 15. Safety / scope

A systems map is not a diagnostic instrument or treatment plan. Do not use it to assign disorders, infer hidden trauma, determine medication changes, or declare that one mechanism explains another person.

## 26. What would change our mind?

The metaphor should be revised or abandoned if it reliably causes learners to misunderstand living systems as deterministic software, obscures embodiment/context, or produces less accurate reasoning than alternative teaching models.
