## 2. Why this matters

“Brain health” attracts two equally unhelpful stories: that decline is inevitable, or that perfect habits can guarantee a perfect brain. Life-course evidence supports neither extreme. Brain health is multidimensional, shaped by biological, behavioral, social, environmental, and health-system factors, and many risks are modifiable without being fully controllable. [F02][F03][F25]

## 3. Learning objectives

- define brain health as a multidimensional life-course concept;
- distinguish risk reduction from prevention guarantees;
- identify broad modifiable and nonmodifiable determinants;
- explain why sleep, physical activity, vascular/metabolic health, learning, safety, environment, and social factors interact;
- avoid blaming individuals for illness or decline.

## 5. Normal human-system baseline

Brain function changes across life. Variation reflects genetics, development, education, health conditions, injury, sensory function, sleep, physical activity, social environment, exposures, and access to resources. Population risk factors shift probability; they do not dictate one person’s outcome.

## 6. Core science

[A/B] WHO frames brain health across cognitive, sensory, social-emotional, behavioral, and motor domains and emphasizes life-course determinants including physical health, safe environments, learning/social connection, and access to services. [F02][F03]

[A/B] Current WHO dementia-risk guidance supports a life-course approach addressing health behaviors, vascular/metabolic conditions, environmental risks, and tailored multidomain approaches while identifying areas where evidence remains insufficient. [F25]

[A/B] Physical activity, tobacco/alcohol exposure, blood pressure, diabetes/metabolic health, sensory impairment, social isolation, sleep, and environmental exposures are among factors considered in contemporary brain-health/risk-reduction guidance; effect strength and intervention evidence differ by factor and population. [F25]

## 7. Brain health is not a supplement aisle

### Physical and vascular health
Brain function depends on blood flow, oxygenation, metabolic health, and broader bodily systems. Managing established health risks can support overall health and may reduce population risk for cognitive decline.

### Sleep and circadian health
Sleep contributes to learning, attention, mood regulation, metabolic and cardiovascular health, and daytime function. Sleep disorders deserve appropriate evaluation rather than generic sleep-hygiene moralizing.

### Learning and cognitive engagement
Education, learning, and meaningful cognitive activity contribute to life-course functioning and may be associated with resilience, but no puzzle or app guarantees prevention of disease.

### Social and environmental conditions
Isolation, poverty, pollution, unsafe environments, discrimination, access barriers, and other structural factors affect health. Brain health is not solely an individual self-discipline project.

### Injury prevention
Seat belts, helmets where appropriate, fall prevention, violence prevention, and safer environments reduce preventable brain injury risk.

## 9. What is known

Some risk is modifiable and some is not. Health behaviors occur within social and economic conditions. Risk reduction can be worthwhile even when it cannot guarantee an outcome.

## 10. What remains uncertain

Optimal combinations, timing, intensity, and individual benefit of multidomain interventions vary. Observational associations can overestimate or underestimate causal effects. New prevention evidence continues to evolve.

## 11. Condition-specific applications

Brain-health practices may matter alongside treatment/rehabilitation for neurological and psychiatric conditions, but general wellness advice is not a substitute for condition-specific care. Medication or treatment changes must be individualized with qualified professionals.

## 12. Combination/comorbidity lens

Cardiovascular disease, diabetes, sleep disorders, sensory loss, chronic pain, psychiatric conditions, brain injury, medication burden, and social disadvantage can interact. The most relevant health priorities differ by person.

## 13. Lived-experience perspective

People can follow excellent health practices and still become ill. Others may face barriers that make idealized recommendations inaccessible. Brain-health education should increase options and understanding, not assign moral credit or blame.

## 15. Safety / scope

This lesson provides general education, not individualized prevention or treatment advice. Do not start, stop, or change medication based on this lesson. New cognitive symptoms, neurological changes, sleep concerns, or other health problems should be discussed with appropriate professionals.
