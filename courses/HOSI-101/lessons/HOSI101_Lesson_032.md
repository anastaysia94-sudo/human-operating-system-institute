## 2. Why this matters

Trying to recall can be a learning event, not merely a measurement event. This lesson teaches a systems-first explanation so that learners can separate mechanism, context, and uncertainty from identity or blame.

## 3. Learning objectives

- Explain how retrieval practice differs from graded testing.
- Use low-stakes recall with feedback.
- Identify limits and equity considerations in implementation.

## 4. Vocabulary

## 6. Core science

[A] Controlled experiments and later educational reviews show that attempting retrieval can strengthen long-term retention more than equivalent additional study in many conditions. [S02][S04][S05]

[A] Retrieval practice can be low stakes or ungraded. Its learning value does not require punishment, surprise, or high-pressure exams. Feedback is especially useful when the learner retrieves incorrectly or incompletely.

[B] Retrieval difficulty must remain productive. If a learner cannot retrieve anything, additional cues, examples, or restudy may be needed before trying again. Transfer to new contexts occurs but is not automatic. [S13]

## 7. Historical context

The field moved from simple “storage box” metaphors toward interacting cognitive systems, computational models, neuropsychology, and network-level neuroscience. Historical models remain useful when treated as models rather than literal maps of a single person's brain.

## 8. Evidence map

- A — Retrieval practice improves delayed retention in many controlled and educational studies. [S02][S05]
- B — Transfer and optimal difficulty depend on task overlap and support. [S13]

HOSI ratings: **A = high confidence; B = moderate confidence; C = limited/mixed; D = preliminary/emerging; E = insufficient.** These are educational labels, not regulatory grades.

## 9. What is known

The highest-confidence statements are those labeled A above and tied to the listed source families.

## 10. What is uncertain

The best mix of free recall, short answer, multiple choice, and feedback varies by content and learner.

## 11. Condition-specific applications

High anxiety or trauma-related reactions may make evaluative formats counterproductive; retrieval can be reframed as private, low-stakes practice.

## 12. Combination/comorbidity panel

For ADHD plus anxiety, use brief retrieval, visible progress, immediate correction, and reduced evaluation pressure.

## 13. Lived-experience perspective

A person may describe the same cognitive phenomenon in different language: “blanking,” “brain fog,” “overload,” “I know it but can't get it out,” or “I never took it in.” HOSI treats these reports as valuable observations while keeping them distinct from diagnostic conclusions and population-level evidence.
