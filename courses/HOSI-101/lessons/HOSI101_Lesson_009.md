## 2. Why this matters

Knowledge creates power. Power without humility is how a useful concept becomes a label, a correlation becomes a verdict, and a confident explanation becomes somebody else’s cage.

The Scholar’s Oath is HOSI’s compact ethical operating rule for learning. It is not a scientific discovery and it is not a professional license. It is a governance commitment about how learners, instructors, researchers, and AI-assisted systems should handle claims about people.

## 3. Learning objectives

- distinguish scholarly ethics from empirical findings;
- explain why uncertainty and correction are obligations rather than embarrassments;
- describe knowledge as stewardship rather than ownership over another person;
- identify conflicts between intellectual confidence and respect for personhood;
- apply the oath to a disputed scientific or clinical claim.

## 4. Vocabulary

scholarship; epistemic humility; stewardship; dignity; correction; provenance; conflict of interest; uncertainty; falsifiability; scope

## 5. Normal human-system baseline

People prefer coherent stories and social certainty. Scholarship asks us to resist the urge to make evidence cleaner, broader, or more personal than it actually is. Research-method guidance emphasizes transparency, replication, explicit limitations, and correction because trustworthy knowledge depends on methods that other people can inspect. [F04][F05][F06]

## 6. Core principles

The HOSI Scholar’s Oath:

1. **I will preserve dignity before labels.** A classification can be useful without becoming a person’s identity.
2. **I will distinguish evidence from interpretation.** A source, an inference, a lived experience, and an institutional value are not interchangeable.
3. **I will state uncertainty.** “We do not know” is sometimes the most accurate sentence available.
4. **I will correct errors visibly.** Scholarship is strengthened, not disgraced, when claims are revised for good reasons.
5. **I will not use confidence as evidence.** Vividness, status, memory confidence, authority, or repetition do not establish factual accuracy.
6. **I will protect scope.** Education is not diagnosis; research findings are not automatic individualized treatment decisions.
7. **I will treat knowledge as stewardship.** Learning about a person does not create ownership over that person’s story.
8. **I will ask what would change my mind.** A claim that cannot be revised has left scholarship and entered doctrine.

## 7. Evidence and philosophy boundary

[A/B] Transparency, reproducibility, replicability, bias appraisal, and explicit methods are established scientific-quality concerns. [F04][F05][F06]

[B/C] Diagnostic language can support communication while also creating risks of stigma or reductionism when labels are treated as complete explanations of personhood. [F09][F10]

The exact wording of the Scholar’s Oath is **HOSI institutional philosophy**. No citation can convert an ethical commitment into a universal empirical law.

## 8. Evidence map

- **A/B —** transparent, reproducible, bias-aware methods support trustworthy science. [F04][F05][F06]
- **B/C —** stigma and reductive classification can affect how people are understood and treated. [F09][F10]
- **Institutional principle —** HOSI requires dignity, visible correction, scope boundaries, and learner ownership of personal narrative.

## 9. What is known

Science is provisional without being arbitrary. Reliable evidence can support strong confidence while still remaining open to correction. Scholarly quality depends not on never being wrong, but on methods that make error detectable and revision possible.

## 10. What remains uncertain

Ethical priorities can conflict. Openness can conflict with privacy; rapid correction can conflict with the need for careful review; plain-language teaching can simplify concepts too far. These tensions require judgment and governance rather than one formula.

## 11. Condition-specific applications

When studying bipolar disorder, ADHD, OCD, PTSD/trauma, brain injury, or any future condition, the oath means:
- avoid turning group averages into individual destiny;
- distinguish symptoms from moral character;
- distinguish treatment evidence from treatment instruction;
- present uncertainty and alternatives;
- preserve personhood even when clinical language is necessary.

## 12. Combination/comorbidity lens

People may meet criteria for more than one condition, may have symptoms that overlap categories, or may have medical, social, developmental, and environmental factors that change how evidence applies. The oath prohibits using one label as a total explanation merely because it is convenient.

## 13. Lived-experience perspective

Lived experience may reveal harms, burdens, goals, barriers, and meanings that formal studies miss. It deserves respect without being converted into universal proof. The learner retains authority over whether a personal story is shared.

## 14. Practical skill / exercise

Take this fictional statement: “People with condition X always struggle with self-control.”

Rewrite it five times:
1. remove identity language;
2. specify population;
3. specify evidence type;
4. add uncertainty/applicability limits;
5. add a dignity-preserving sentence explaining what the claim does **not** establish.

## 15. Safety / scope

This lesson teaches academic ethics. It does not confer clinical, legal, research-ethics, or professional credentials. It does not diagnose anyone and does not authorize the learner to override professional or personal consent boundaries.

## 17. Reflection

Which is harder for you: saying “I was wrong,” saying “I do not know,” or saying “this evidence may not apply to this person”? Why?

## 18. Discussion prompt

Can scholarship be both confident and humble? Give an example where strong evidence justifies confidence while uncertainty still matters.

## 20. Homework

Choose one scientific or health claim. Write a 250–400 word “oath-compliant” summary that separates evidence, interpretation, uncertainty, lived experience, and scope.

## 21. Scholar challenge

Find a published correction, retraction, guideline update, or major scientific revision. Explain how the change illustrates scholarship as a self-correcting process rather than a failure of knowledge itself.

## 22. Instructor answer key / rubric

Strong work clearly separates empirical claims from values, includes meaningful uncertainty, avoids identity reduction, and identifies what evidence would justify revision. Do not award points for solemn-sounding prose that never states a testable boundary.

## 26. What would change our mind?

The wording of the oath should change if it creates foreseeable harm, conflicts with stronger ethical guidance, obscures scientific uncertainty, or fails learner testing. Empirical supporting statements should change when better evidence warrants it.
