## 2. Why this matters

Orientation is not the decorative lobby before the “real science.” It establishes the rules that determine whether later neuroscience and clinical material becomes useful education or an overconfident pile of vocabulary.

This review asks learners to demonstrate the habits HOSI expects throughout the course: define the claim, identify the system, separate evidence from interpretation, state uncertainty, preserve scope, and explain what would change the conclusion.

## 3. Learning objectives

- synthesize the nine Orientation lessons into one decision process;
- distinguish diagnosis, identity, system models, evidence, and lived experience;
- construct a bounded systems explanation without claiming causality unsupported by evidence;
- appraise a claim using evidence-quality dimensions;
- apply the Scholar’s Oath to a realistic educational scenario.

## 4. Vocabulary

## 5. Normal human-system baseline

Humans learn by connecting new material with prior structure, retrieving it, applying it, and correcting misunderstandings. A review should therefore test relationships among ideas rather than reward recognition of isolated definitions.

## 6. Core synthesis

### Principle 1 — A diagnosis may be useful without becoming identity
A diagnostic construct can support communication, research, services, and care while remaining incomplete as a description of a person. [F09][F10]

### Principle 2 — Human functioning is multi-system
Attention, memory, emotion, behavior, sleep, relationships, development, physical health, and environment interact. A systems map is a structured hypothesis, not proof of causation. [F02][F03][F07][F08]

### Principle 3 — Learning changes biological systems, but plasticity has limits
Experience can alter neural function and structure; “plastic” does not mean infinitely rewritable. [F11][F12]

### Principle 4 — Science is inspectable and revisable
Methods, assumptions, data, analysis, replication, and correction matter. [F04][F06]

### Principle 5 — Evidence confidence is claim-specific
Risk of bias, consistency, precision, directness, and reporting bias matter more than a prestige label attached to one paper. [F05]

### Principle 6 — Scholarship includes dignity and scope
HOSI separates institutional values from empirical claims and requires visible uncertainty and correction.

## 7. Orientation decision sequence

When you encounter a claim, ask:
1. **What exactly is being claimed?**
2. **What system(s) are involved?**
3. **What is the evidence type?**
4. **How direct is it for this population and outcome?**
5. **What alternative explanations exist?**
6. **What remains uncertain?**
7. **What does the claim not prove?**
8. **Does applying it to an individual exceed the evidence?**
9. **What would change our mind?**

## 8. Evidence map

This lesson introduces no new empirical claim. It synthesizes the evidence and institutional boundaries already documented in Lessons 1–9 and source registry [F02]–[F12].

## 9. What is known

Strong scholarship can be rigorous without pretending to eliminate uncertainty. Systems models can organize complexity without proving every arrow. Diagnostic language can be useful without becoming identity. Neuroplasticity is real without being magical. Evidence appraisal is stronger when tied to a specific claim.

## 10. What remains uncertain

HOSI’s teaching sequence itself must still be tested with learners. A coherent curriculum architecture does not prove that every learner will understand, retain, transfer, or benefit from it.

## 11. Condition-specific applications

Later condition modules will use the Orientation rules to prevent common errors:
- “this diagnosis explains everything”;
- “this brain region causes the condition”;
- “this treatment worked in a study, therefore everyone should use it”;
- “this lived experience is universal”;
- “this mechanism proves clinical benefit.”

## 12. Combination/comorbidity lens

When conditions overlap, the orientation framework becomes more important, not less. Learners should distinguish overlapping symptoms, shared mechanisms, separate diagnoses, treatment interactions, and contextual factors rather than forcing one master explanation.

## 13. Lived-experience perspective

A learner may recognize personal experiences in course material. Recognition can be meaningful without functioning as self-diagnosis. Personal reflection is optional, and fictional cases are acceptable substitutes throughout HOSI.

## 14. Practical skill / exercise

### Case
A headline says: “Scientists found the brain network responsible for procrastination, proving that procrastinators have a biological disorder.”

Create a response using the nine-question Orientation decision sequence. Your response should identify at least four unsupported leaps.

## 15. Safety / scope

This review is educational and does not diagnose, screen, grade mental health, or determine whether a learner has any condition. Learners may decline personal disclosure. Instructors should grade reasoning, not biography.

## 19. Knowledge check / quiz

2. Why does a causal-loop diagram not prove causality?
3. What is one limit of neuroplasticity language?
4. Distinguish replicability from reproducibility.
5. Name three evidence-quality dimensions.
6. Why is lived experience neither disposable nor universal proof?
7. What is the difference between philosophy and empirical evidence?
8. What does “what would change our mind?” accomplish?

## 20. Homework

Write a 500–700 word analysis of one public neuroscience or mental-health claim. Use the nine-question decision sequence and include a corrected version of the claim that preserves what the evidence can actually support.

## 21. Scholar challenge

Create a one-page “Orientation field card” that another learner could use while reading a paper, news article, social post, or guideline. It must fit on one page without deleting uncertainty or scope.

## 22. Instructor answer key / rubric

Grade across five dimensions: claim definition, systems reasoning, evidence appraisal, uncertainty/scope, and scholarly ethics. A high-scoring answer identifies both what evidence supports and what it does not support. Do not reward jargon density.
