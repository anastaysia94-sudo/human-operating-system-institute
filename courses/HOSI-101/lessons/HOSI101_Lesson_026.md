## 2. Why this matters

Remembering is an active reconstruction from stored information, cues, context, and current knowledge—not a literal replay. This lesson teaches a systems-first explanation so that learners can separate mechanism, context, and uncertainty from identity or blame.

## 3. Learning objectives

- Explain why episodic memory is reconstructive.
- Describe the misinformation effect without implying all memory is unreliable.
- Apply cautious interviewing and documentation principles.

## 4. Vocabulary

reconstructive memory; misinformation effect; source monitoring; suggestibility; confidence

## 6. Core science

[A] Decades of experimental research show that misleading post-event information can alter later reports and sometimes memory judgments. This is commonly called the misinformation effect. [S11]

[A/B] Susceptibility varies across people and conditions. A 2024 systematic review found that several cognitive abilities were associated with reduced susceptibility in the included adult studies, but no trait made people immune. [S12]

[A] Distortion research does not justify the claim that “memory is useless” or that every inconsistency proves fabrication. Confidence, vividness, and accuracy are related imperfectly and should be evaluated with context and corroborating evidence.

[B] Correcting misinformation can improve memory and beliefs, but correction effects can weaken with time and depend on source recollection and integration.

## 7. Historical context

The field moved from simple “storage box” metaphors toward interacting cognitive systems, computational models, neuropsychology, and network-level neuroscience. Historical models remain useful when treated as models rather than literal maps of a single person's brain.

## 8. Evidence map

- A — Post-event misinformation can influence later memory reports. [S11]
- B — Individual differences may alter susceptibility, but protection is incomplete and evidence is heterogeneous. [S12]

HOSI ratings: **A = high confidence; B = moderate confidence; C = limited/mixed; D = preliminary/emerging; E = insufficient.** These are educational labels, not regulatory grades.

## 9. What is known

The highest-confidence statements are those labeled A above and tied to the listed source families.

## 10. What is uncertain

Research does not provide a reliable way to inspect one recollection and determine whether it is accurate solely from how it feels.

## 11. Condition-specific applications

Trauma, stress, and brain injury can complicate memory, but they do not permit blanket assumptions that memories are either perfectly accurate or inherently false.

## 12. Combination/comorbidity panel

For autobiographical Atlas work, separate memory confidence from factual verification; record corroborating evidence and alternative interpretations.

## 13. Lived-experience perspective

A person may describe the same cognitive phenomenon in different language: “blanking,” “brain fog,” “overload,” “I know it but can't get it out,” or “I never took it in.” HOSI treats these reports as valuable observations while keeping them distinct from diagnostic conclusions and population-level evidence.

## 14. Practical skill / exercise

Rewrite a remembered event in three columns: what you remember directly, what you learned later from others/media, and what is uncertain.

## 15. Safety / scope

## 21. Instructor answer key / rubric

Reject both extremes: “memory is a recording” and “memory is worthless.” Strong answers emphasize source monitoring and corroboration.

Grade for conceptual accuracy, evidence calibration, reasoning, application, clarity, and scope/ethics. Do **not** grade a learner's personal beliefs, diagnosis, treatment choices, or willingness to disclose lived experience.
