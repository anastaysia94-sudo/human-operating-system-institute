## 2. Why this matters

“Studies show” is one of the internet’s favorite disguises. It can introduce excellent evidence, a single tiny experiment, a badly interpreted observational study, or a press release that got ambitious after lunch.

Evidence literacy means asking not only **what source type is this?** but **how well does this evidence answer this exact claim?**

## 3. Learning objectives

- distinguish study design from overall evidence quality;
- explain risk of bias, directness, precision, consistency/heterogeneity, and reporting bias;
- identify why systematic reviews can be strong or weak;
- distinguish statistical association from clinical or practical importance;
- calibrate confidence to a specific claim rather than to an entire topic.

## 4. Vocabulary

risk of bias; precision; confidence interval; directness; applicability; consistency; heterogeneity; reporting bias; systematic review; meta-analysis; randomized trial; observational study

## 5. Normal human-system baseline

People often use shortcuts when judging evidence: newer must be better, bigger must be more important, randomized must be perfect, peer reviewed must be true, or systematic review must sit forever at the top of a pyramid.

Those shortcuts can orient beginners, but serious appraisal asks whether the design and execution fit the question.

## 6. Core science / method

[A] National Academies systematic-review standards emphasize appraisal of **risk of bias, consistency, precision, directness, and reporting bias** across a body of evidence. [F05]

[A/B] Evidence hierarchies can be useful because some designs provide stronger protections against particular biases, but hierarchy alone is insufficient. The appropriate design depends on the question, and low-quality execution can weaken any design. [F05]

[B] Recent meta-research continues to document concerns about reporting, analytic flexibility, sample size, bias, and reproducibility across health research. [F06]

## 7. Study types and the questions they answer

### Randomized trials
Useful for estimating causal treatment/intervention effects when randomization is feasible and well implemented. They may still have attrition, measurement, adherence, blinding, selective-reporting, generalizability, or power problems.

### Observational studies
Often essential for risk factors, prognosis, rare harms, long-term outcomes, and questions that cannot be randomized. Confounding and selection require careful attention.

### Diagnostic studies
Ask how a test performs relative to a reference process; they require attention to spectrum, thresholds, verification, and intended use.

### Mechanistic/basic science
Can reveal how biological processes work but may be indirect for clinical outcomes.

### Systematic reviews/meta-analyses
Can synthesize bodies of evidence if search, selection, appraisal, extraction, and synthesis are rigorous. A meta-analysis cannot magically repair biased or irrelevant input studies.

### Guidelines/consensus statements
Integrate evidence with values, feasibility, harms, applicability, and professional judgment. Recommendations are not raw study results.

## 8. Evidence map

- **A —** Evidence appraisal should address bias, consistency, precision, directness, and reporting bias rather than source label alone. [F05]
- **A/B —** Transparency and methodological choices materially affect evidence trustworthiness. [F04][F06]

## 9. What is known

Confidence is claim-specific. The same paper may strongly support one statement and weakly support another. A trial of short-term symptom change may not directly establish long-term functioning; a mechanistic imaging result may not establish treatment efficacy.

## 10. What remains uncertain

Evidence-grading systems differ across organizations and disciplines. Judgments about directness, bias, and clinical importance can involve expert interpretation even when explicit frameworks are used.

## 11. Condition-specific applications

Condition study paths will encounter evidence about:
- diagnosis/classification;
- mechanisms;
- prognosis;
- psychotherapy;
- medication;
- rehabilitation;
- lived experience;
- comorbidity.

Do not use one evidence category to answer a different question. A mechanism paper is not automatically a treatment trial; a treatment trial is not automatically a diagnostic test.

## 12. Combination/comorbidity lens

A study may exclude people with several co-occurring conditions. That can make the evidence less direct for learners interested in real-world combinations. HOSI should explicitly record inclusion/exclusion and applicability rather than quietly generalizing.

## 13. Lived-experience perspective

Lived experience can reveal outcomes that researchers did not measure. It can identify acceptability, burden, practical barriers, and meaningful goals. It should be integrated as its own evidence domain rather than either dismissed or promoted into universal causal proof.

## 14. Practical skill / exercise

Appraise this fictional evidence claim:

“An app improved attention.”

Ask:
1. Who used the app?
2. Compared with what?
3. What did “attention” mean operationally?
4. How large and precise was the effect?
5. Was the outcome measured immediately or later?
6. Did people know their condition/intervention?
7. Was the result pre-specified?
8. Does it transfer to daily functioning?

## 15. Safety / scope

This lesson is not a diagnostic assessment. Evidence ratings, study findings, or classroom appraisal exercises must not be used to diagnose a learner or another person.

Evidence ratings in HOSI are educational summaries. They do not replace clinical guidelines, regulatory review, individualized risk-benefit decisions, or professional judgment.

A high evidence grade does not mean a treatment is correct for every person; a low grade does not prove something is impossible.

## 20. Homework

Choose one research claim from a reputable article or guideline. Complete a five-domain appraisal: risk of bias, directness, precision, consistency, and reporting bias. Include at least one reason your confidence might change.

## 21. Scholar challenge

Find two evidence-grading systems (for example GRADE and another field-specific approach). Compare what they assess and where judgment enters the process.

## 22. Instructor answer key / rubric

Strong work distinguishes design from execution, applies evidence domains to an exact claim, and avoids treating study-design labels as automatic truth scores.
