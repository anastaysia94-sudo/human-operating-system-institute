## 2. Why this matters

Emotion changes what people attend to and what later feels important, but memorable does not mean perfectly recorded. This lesson teaches a systems-first explanation so that learners can separate mechanism, context, and uncertainty from identity or blame.

## 3. Learning objectives

- Explain how emotional arousal can influence memory.
- Reject the myth that emotional memories are automatically accurate.
- Distinguish salience, vividness, and factual precision.

## 4. Vocabulary

emotional arousal; salience; amygdala; consolidation; vividness; valence

## 5. Normal human-system baseline

Emotion changes what people attend to and what later feels important, but memorable does not mean perfectly recorded.

## 6. Core science

[A] Emotional arousal can modulate memory processes through interacting neural and hormonal systems, including amygdala-linked modulation of distributed memory circuits. [S18]

[A/B] Emotion can enhance memory for some central or salient information while other details remain weak or distorted. The direction and size of effects vary with intensity, timing, task, and person.

[A] Vividness and confidence are not guarantees of accuracy. Emotion-memory research should therefore never be used to declare a disputed autobiographical memory true or false solely because it feels intense.

## 7. Historical context

The field moved from simple “storage box” metaphors toward interacting cognitive systems, computational models, neuropsychology, and network-level neuroscience. Historical models remain useful when treated as models rather than literal maps of a single person's brain.

## 8. Evidence map

- A — Emotional arousal modulates memory through interacting neural systems. [S18]
- B — Effects on specific details vary by task, timing, and arousal.

HOSI ratings: **A = high confidence; B = moderate confidence; C = limited/mixed; D = preliminary/emerging; E = insufficient.** These are educational labels, not regulatory grades.

## 9. What is known

Emotion can change encoding, consolidation, and later retrieval; effects are selective rather than uniformly beneficial.

## 10. What is uncertain

The field continues to study why emotion enhances some aspects of memory while narrowing or disrupting others.

## 11. Condition-specific applications

In PTSD, intrusive memories and concentration problems can occur, but not everyone exposed to trauma develops PTSD. [S15]

## 12. Combination/comorbidity panel

OCD, PTSD, bipolar mood states, and ADHD may each alter attention or salience; avoid one-size-fits-all neurochemical stories.

## 13. Lived-experience perspective

A vivid or emotionally intense memory can matter deeply to a person even when some factual details remain uncertain. HOSI respects the experience while separating meaning, memory confidence, corroboration, and population-level evidence.

## 14. Practical skill / exercise

Take a non-sensitive emotional event and separate what was central, what was peripheral, what was learned later, and what remains uncertain.

## 15. Safety / scope

This lesson is educational and not a diagnosis, treatment plan, medication recommendation, forensic memory assessment, or substitute for qualified medical/mental-health care. Learners should not be required to disclose traumatic or sensitive memories. Urgent neurological or safety concerns require appropriate professional or emergency care.

## 21. Instructor answer key / rubric

Require both modulation and uncertainty; reject “emotion makes memory perfect.” Full-credit answers distinguish subjective vividness from independent factual verification.

Grade for conceptual accuracy, evidence calibration, reasoning, application, clarity, and scope/ethics. Do **not** grade a learner's personal beliefs, diagnosis, treatment choices, or willingness to disclose lived experience.
