## 2. Why this matters

Introductory neuroscience often swings between two bad extremes: every behavior is assigned to one colored brain blob, or the learner is told the brain is so interconnected that location barely matters. Neither is good enough.

Brain regions differ in anatomy, cell types, inputs, outputs, and functional specialization. At the same time, complex perception, cognition, emotion, movement, and regulation usually depend on interactions across distributed circuits and networks. [F01][F13][F19]

## 3. Learning objectives

- identify the broad organizational divisions of the human brain;
- distinguish cerebral cortex, major subcortical structures, cerebellum, and brainstem;
- explain specialization without reducing complex functions to one region;
- distinguish an anatomical map from a functional explanation;
- state why group-level neuroimaging findings are not individual diagnostic tests.

## 4. Vocabulary

cerebrum; cortex; white matter; subcortical; thalamus; basal ganglia; hypothalamus; hippocampal formation; amygdala; cerebellum; brainstem; network; connectivity; lateralization

## 5. Normal human-system baseline

All healthy brains contain recurring large-scale anatomical structures, but size, shape, connectivity, functional organization, development, and recruitment vary across people and across tasks. Normal variation is not automatically pathology.

## 6. Core science

[A] The central nervous system includes brain and spinal cord. Within the brain, broad divisions include the cerebrum/cerebral cortex and underlying white matter, deep subcortical structures, cerebellum, and brainstem. [F01]

[A/B] Different regions have specialized anatomical and physiological properties, yet higher-order functions are typically supported by interacting networks rather than one structure operating alone. [F13][F19]

[B] Intensively sampled human neuroimaging shows broad common organization alongside meaningful person-to-person differences in cortical network layout. [F13]

## 7. A practical organizational map

### Cerebral cortex
The folded outer sheet of the cerebrum. It includes primary sensory/motor regions and extensive association cortex involved in integrating information across domains.

### White matter
Bundles of axons that connect cortical and subcortical areas. White matter organization helps constrain how information can travel through the brain.

### Thalamus
A collection of nuclei deeply involved in communication among cortical and subcortical systems. It is not merely a passive relay.

### Basal ganglia
Interconnected nuclei important for action selection, movement, learning, reward-related processes, and habit-related computations.

### Hypothalamus
Small but highly influential structure involved in homeostatic and endocrine regulation, autonomic control, motivated behavior, and coordination with broader brain/body systems.

### Hippocampal formation
Critical to several forms of learning, memory, spatial/context processing, and relational representation.

### Amygdala
A family of nuclei involved in salience, learning, threat/reward processing, and interactions with memory, autonomic, and cognitive systems. It is not a universal “fear center.”

### Cerebellum
Classically associated with coordination and motor learning; modern work also supports cognitive and affective contributions. [F17][F18]

### Brainstem
Midbrain, pons, and medulla contain nuclei essential for arousal, autonomic regulation, sensorimotor functions, cranial-nerve functions, and broad integration with cortical systems. [F19][F20]

- **A —** Major anatomical divisions and broad structural descriptions. [F01]
- **A/B —** Complex function depends on distributed interactions as well as regional specialization. [F13][F19]

## 9. What is known

Anatomical organization matters. Damage to different structures can produce different patterns of impairment. Yet the behavioral meaning of a region depends partly on its connections, task context, timing, development, and the rest of the network.

## 10. What remains uncertain

The field continues refining parcellations and network definitions. Different imaging methods, tasks, analyses, and theoretical frameworks can produce different boundaries. There is no final brain atlas that converts a scan into a complete explanation of a person.

## 11. Condition-specific applications

Condition-specific research may report average differences in regions or networks, but these findings rarely function as standalone diagnostic markers. HOSI will treat neuroimaging as population-level evidence unless validated individual diagnostic performance is specifically established.

## 12. Combination/comorbidity lens

Co-occurring conditions, medication exposure, sleep, development, injury, cardiovascular factors, substance exposure, and many other variables can influence brain measurements. A group difference cannot automatically be assigned to one diagnosis.

## 13. Lived-experience perspective

People may describe feeling that “one part of my brain is broken.” That language can communicate distress, but it should not be treated as a literal neuroanatomical conclusion without appropriate evidence.

## 15. Safety / scope

This lesson is not a neurological examination, diagnosis, or scan-reading course. New focal weakness, loss of consciousness, severe sudden headache, seizure, acute confusion, or other possible neurological emergencies require appropriate urgent medical evaluation rather than classroom interpretation.
