## 2. Why this matters

Popular diagrams often present “the scientific method” as a neat school-lab recipe. Real science is messier. Different fields use different designs, exploratory work can precede formal hypotheses, unexpected results can matter, and methods are revised as instruments and theories improve.

The core discipline is not one sacred sequence. It is making claims answerable to evidence and making methods clear enough that others can inspect, reproduce where appropriate, challenge, and extend them.

## 3. Learning objectives

- distinguish observation, hypothesis, prediction, measurement, analysis, and interpretation;
- explain why a hypothesis should expose itself to possible disconfirmation;
- distinguish reproducibility from replicability using the National Academies definitions;
- identify common reasons studies can disagree;
- explain why scientific revision is a strength rather than proof that science “failed.”

## 4. Vocabulary

observation; hypothesis; prediction; operational definition; variable; measurement; confounding; reproducibility; replicability; transparency; falsifiability; revision

## 5. Normal human-system baseline

Humans notice patterns and build explanations quickly. That capacity is useful and also vulnerable to confirmation bias, selective memory, overconfidence, and seeing causation in coincidence. Scientific methods create structured friction against some of those errors.

## 6. Core science / method

[A] The National Academies define **reproducibility** as obtaining consistent computational results using the same data, computational steps, methods, code, and conditions of analysis; **replicability** refers to obtaining consistent results across studies answering the same scientific question using new data. [F04]

[A/B] Transparency, complete reporting, adequate sample size/power, appropriate analysis, and clear methods are among factors associated with more trustworthy/reproducible health-science research. [F04][F06]

A study can fail to replicate for many reasons: the original effect may be weak or false; the replication may differ meaningfully; populations or contexts may differ; measurement may be noisy; chance may matter; or a true effect may have boundary conditions.

## 7. Historical context

Science did not emerge from one universal checklist. Experimental, observational, mathematical, qualitative, clinical, historical, and computational sciences answer different questions with different tools.

Modern open-science and reproducibility movements have emphasized preregistration, shared protocols/data/code where ethically possible, registered reports, transparent reporting, and replication. These practices reduce some opportunities for undisclosed flexibility but do not make research error-proof.

## 8. Evidence map

- **A —** Transparent methods and independent replication/reproduction are central to scientific self-correction. [F04]
- **B —** Multiple modifiable research practices are associated with reproducibility and reliability. [F06]
- **Context-dependent —** The ideal study design depends on the question; there is no single experiment type that answers every scientific question.

## 9. What is known

Science becomes more trustworthy when methods, data provenance, analyses, limitations, and uncertainty are visible enough for scrutiny.

## 10. What remains uncertain

No procedural checklist can eliminate bias. Some questions cannot be randomized for ethical or practical reasons. Replication can itself be poorly designed. The interpretation of conflicting evidence requires subject-matter and methods expertise.

## 11. Condition-specific applications

When reading about ADHD, bipolar disorder, OCD, PTSD, TBI, or any other condition, ask:
- What exact question was studied?
- Who was included/excluded?
- How was the condition defined?
- What comparison group existed?
- What outcome was measured?
- Was the study designed to establish association, prediction, mechanism, or treatment effect?

## 12. Combination/comorbidity lens

Clinical studies may exclude complex comorbidity to obtain a cleaner sample. That can improve internal control while reducing directness to real-world patients with multiple conditions. Exclusion criteria therefore matter when generalizing results.

## 13. Lived-experience perspective

A lived observation can generate a research question: “This seems to happen after X.” Science asks how to test whether that relationship is systematic, causal, generalizable, or explained by something else.

Lived experience is not devalued by testing. Testing changes the type of claim being made.

## 14. Practical skill / exercise

Convert this observation into a testable structure:

“Students seem to remember more when they quiz themselves.”

Write:
1. a hypothesis;
2. a prediction;
3. independent variable;
4. outcome measure;
5. comparison condition;
6. one confound;
7. one result that would weaken your hypothesis.

## 15. Safety / scope

This lesson is not a diagnostic method, and a classroom experiment or research-design exercise must not be used to diagnose a medical, psychiatric, neurological, or learning condition.

Do not perform risky experiments on yourself or others to answer classroom questions. Do not change prescribed medication, induce sleep deprivation, alter substance use, or change medical treatment as a student experiment. Human research requires ethical safeguards appropriate to risk.

## 18. Discussion prompt

If a well-conducted study fails to replicate another well-conducted study, what questions should scientists ask before deciding one study is “wrong”?

## 19. Knowledge check / quiz

1. What is the difference between reproducibility and replicability under the National Academies definitions?
2. What is an operational definition?
3. Why should a hypothesis specify evidence that could weaken it?
4. Name two reasons studies may disagree.
5. Does a replication failure automatically prove fraud?

## 20. Homework

Take one health or psychology headline. Locate the original study if possible and write a one-page methods card: design, sample, exposure/intervention, comparison, outcome, major limitation, and what the study cannot establish.

## 21. Scholar challenge

Compare exploratory and confirmatory research. Explain why both are useful but why pretending an exploratory finding was pre-specified can distort evidence.
