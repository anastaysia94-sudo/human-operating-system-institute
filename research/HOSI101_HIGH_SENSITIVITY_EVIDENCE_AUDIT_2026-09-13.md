# HOSI-101 High-Sensitivity Evidence Audit — Lessons 38–39

**Audit date:** 2026-09-13  
**Scope:** HOSI-101 Lesson 38 (Memory After Trauma) and Lesson 39 (Memory After Brain Injury)  
**Status:** AI-assisted evidence-hardening pass complete; independent human subject-matter, clinical/safety, lived-experience, accessibility, and full-text citation review remain pending.

## Purpose

This file records a focused claim-to-source audit for two lessons where overstatement can cause particular harm. It does not certify the lessons as final, reviewed, clinically approved, or publishable.

The audit follows HOSI Evidence Policy 1.0: evidence strength belongs to the **claim**, not permanently to the source; population, design, outcome, uncertainty, contradictory evidence, and applicability must remain visible.

## Lesson 38 — Memory After Trauma

### Claim T38-01 — Post-trauma reactions are heterogeneous and trauma exposure does not automatically equal PTSD

- **Claim:** People can experience sleep, concentration, repeated-thought, avoidance, and arousal changes after traumatic events; many people improve, and trauma exposure alone does not establish PTSD.
- **Primary source family:** NIMH federal health education pages already cited as S15/S22.
- **Source type:** Authoritative federal health source.
- **Population:** General public / trauma-exposed populations as summarized by NIMH.
- **Design:** Evidence-synthesis/public-health guidance, not a single trial.
- **Comparator:** Not applicable.
- **Outcome:** Educational description of common reactions and PTSD scope.
- **HOSI rating:** A for broad educational framing.
- **Limitations:** Not designed to establish a specific individual's diagnosis, prognosis, memory accuracy, or treatment need.
- **Contradictory evidence:** None identified that would justify the opposite universal claim; prevalence and symptom trajectories vary by population and exposure.
- **Applicability:** Appropriate for non-diagnostic education only.
- **Reviewer:** ChatGPT metadata/claim check; independent human reviewer pending.
- **Review date:** 2026-09-13.

### Claim T38-02 — There is no single universal “trauma-memory pattern”

- **Claim:** Trauma-related autobiographical-memory findings vary by trauma type, population, clinical context, and measurement; the lesson must not teach that all trauma memories are always fragmented, unusually exact, or impaired in the same way.
- **Source:** Borrelli G, Lamberti Zanardi A, Scognamiglio C, Cinquegrana V, Perrella R. *The relationship between childhood interpersonal and non-interpersonal trauma and autobiographical memory: a systematic review.* Front Psychol. 2024;15:1328835.
- **Source type:** Systematic review (48 included studies).
- **Year:** 2024.
- **Population:** Children, adolescents, and adults with reported childhood interpersonal and/or non-interpersonal trauma; clinical and non-clinical samples.
- **Design:** Systematic review following PRISMA methods.
- **Comparator:** Patterns differed across trauma type and clinical/non-clinical samples rather than one uniform comparator.
- **Outcome:** Autobiographical-memory measures; results were not uniform across trauma categories.
- **HOSI rating:** B/C for broad trauma-memory generalization; the evidence supports heterogeneity more strongly than a universal memory phenotype.
- **Limitations:** Childhood-trauma focus; many included studies were retrospective; measures and trauma definitions varied; not a direct test of factual accuracy of a particular autobiographical report.
- **Contradictory evidence:** The review itself reports conflicting results and less consistent associations for non-interpersonal trauma. Those differences are part of the conclusion, not noise to hide.
- **Applicability:** Supports narrowing the lesson's language. Does not prove that a specific trauma memory is accurate, inaccurate, fragmented, or recovered.
- **Verified DOI:** 10.3389/fpsyg.2024.1328835
- **Verified URL:** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38298520/
- **Reviewer:** ChatGPT evidence-hardening pass; independent human full-text reviewer pending.
- **Review date:** 2026-09-13.

### Claim T38-03 — Recovered-memory reports cannot be verified from subjective qualities alone, and suggestive practices require caution

- **Claim:** Reports of previously inaccessible or unconsidered traumatic memories occur in multiple contexts, but prevalence estimates depend strongly on definition and sample; no unique cognitive mechanism by itself verifies a recovered memory. HOSI should avoid suggestive memory-recovery procedures and should not label a report automatically true or false from confidence, vividness, distress, or recovery context alone.
- **Source:** Carey OG, Dempsey M, Minihane K, Murphy G. *When, where, and how often do individuals recover memories of traumatic experiences? A systematic review.* Memory. Published online 2025 Dec 22; print 2026;34(2):238-259.
- **Source type:** Systematic review (42 post-2000 studies).
- **Year:** 2025 online / 2026 print.
- **Population:** Diverse samples including documented-abuse cohorts, self-report survivor samples, therapy attendees, general-population samples, and clinician reports.
- **Design:** Systematic review.
- **Comparator:** Prevalence and context differed by operational definition, sample, and recovery setting.
- **Outcome:** Reported recovered-memory prevalence and contextual/cognitive explanations.
- **HOSI rating:** B for the educational caution; exact prevalence estimates should not be generalized across populations.
- **Limitations:** Heterogeneous definitions and samples; recovered-memory prevalence is not the same question as factual accuracy of an individual report.
- **Contradictory evidence:** Variation across study types is substantial. The review does not support a single universal recovered-memory mechanism or a blanket claim that all recovered memories are false.
- **Applicability:** Strongly relevant to HOSI safety design, Atlas provenance, non-suggestive reflection, and factual-uncertainty labels.
- **Verified DOI:** 10.1080/09658211.2025.2601699
- **Verified URL:** https://doi.org/10.1080/09658211.2025.2601699
- **Reviewer:** ChatGPT evidence-hardening pass; independent human full-text/forensic-memory reviewer pending.
- **Review date:** 2026-09-13.

### Lesson 38 revision consequence

The 2026-09-13 revision:

1. Removed language that could be read as implying a universal fragmented-trauma-memory phenotype.
2. Added systematic-review evidence for heterogeneity.
3. Added systematic-review evidence on recovered-memory contexts and definitional variability.
4. Explicitly prohibited suggestive memory-recovery exercises within HOSI coursework.
5. Preserved the distinction between lived meaning, memory confidence, corroboration, and factual verification.

## Lesson 39 — Memory After Brain Injury

### Claim T39-01 — TBI can affect multiple cognitive systems, and education cannot infer an individual prognosis

- **Claim:** Mild through severe TBI can be associated with attention, processing-speed, memory, executive, sleep, emotional, and functional changes; severity labels alone do not determine an individual day-to-day profile or prognosis.
- **Primary source family:** CDC TBI guidance already cited as S16/S17.
- **Source type:** Authoritative federal health source.
- **Population:** General public / people with mild, moderate, or severe TBI as summarized by CDC.
- **Design:** Public-health guidance/evidence synthesis.
- **Comparator:** Not applicable.
- **Outcome:** Symptom, function, recovery, and safety education.
- **HOSI rating:** A for broad educational/safety framing.
- **Limitations:** Not an individualized neuropsychological assessment, prognosis model, or rehabilitation plan.
- **Applicability:** Appropriate for non-diagnostic education and escalation guidance.
- **Reviewer:** ChatGPT metadata/claim check; independent human clinical reviewer pending.
- **Review date:** 2026-09-13.

### Claim T39-02 — Evidence-based memory rehabilitation can use individualized internal and external compensatory strategies

- **Claim:** For memory impairment after moderate-to-severe TBI, contemporary cognitive-rehabilitation guidance supports individualized compensatory strategies; external aids, including electronic reminder technology, can be particularly important for severe memory impairment.
- **Source:** Velikonja D, Ponsford J, Janzen S, et al. *INCOG 2.0 Guidelines for Cognitive Rehabilitation Following Traumatic Brain Injury, Part V: Memory.* J Head Trauma Rehabil. 2023;38(1):83-102.
- **Source type:** International expert clinical-practice guideline update based on evidence review.
- **Year:** 2023.
- **Population:** Primarily adults with moderate-to-severe TBI and episodic/prospective memory impairment addressed by the guideline evidence base.
- **Design:** Guideline update with evidence review and graded recommendations.
- **Comparator:** Intervention evidence varies by strategy and underlying studies.
- **Outcome:** Memory rehabilitation and functional use of internal/external strategies.
- **HOSI rating:** A/B for the guideline-level claim that individualized compensatory strategies are supported; this does not mean every strategy has equal evidence or works for every learner.
- **Limitations:** Guideline scope centers on TBI rehabilitation, especially moderate-to-severe injury; it is not a recommendation for an unassessed HOSI learner to self-prescribe rehabilitation.
- **Contradictory evidence:** Heterogeneity in intervention trials means guideline support for a strategy class should not be converted into a universal treatment-effect claim.
- **Applicability:** Supports teaching what compensatory rehabilitation is and why contextual tailoring matters. Actual rehabilitation choices belong to qualified clinicians and patients.
- **Verified DOI:** 10.1097/HTR.0000000000000837
- **Verified URL:** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36594861/
- **Reviewer:** ChatGPT evidence-hardening pass; independent human rehabilitation reviewer pending.
- **Review date:** 2026-09-13.

### Claim T39-03 — Compensatory cognitive-training trial outcomes remain heterogeneous

- **Claim:** Compensatory cognitive training is promising, but randomized-study results are heterogeneous and should not be marketed as a guaranteed cognitive restoration method.
- **Source:** Alashram AR. *Compensatory cognitive training for people with traumatic brain injury: A systematic review of randomized controlled trial.* Appl Neuropsychol Adult. Published online 2024 Jan 29; print 2025;32(6):1794-1802.
- **Source type:** Systematic review of randomized controlled studies.
- **Year:** 2024 online / 2025 print.
- **Population:** People with TBI in 8 included studies (total n=615); participant and protocol characteristics varied.
- **Design:** Systematic review of randomized controlled studies.
- **Comparator:** Varied across included trials.
- **Outcome:** Cognitive outcomes after compensatory cognitive training.
- **HOSI rating:** B/C for broad efficacy claims because findings were heterogeneous/inconsistent; useful as a calibration source alongside INCOG guidance.
- **Limitations:** Small number of studies; heterogeneous protocols and outcomes; limited representation noted in the review.
- **Contradictory evidence:** The review's inconsistent outcomes are intentionally retained as a counterweight to overgeneralizing guideline recommendations.
- **Applicability:** Supports the lesson's distinction between evidence-based strategy categories and individualized expected benefit.
- **Verified DOI:** 10.1080/23279095.2024.2306133
- **Verified URL:** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38285433/
- **Reviewer:** ChatGPT evidence-hardening pass; independent human full-text/rehabilitation reviewer pending.
- **Review date:** 2026-09-13.

### Lesson 39 revision consequence

The 2026-09-13 revision:

1. Added INCOG 2.0 memory-rehabilitation guidance.
2. Added a systematic review that documents heterogeneous compensatory-training outcomes.
3. Reframed external memory aids as evidence-supported strategy options that require functional/contextual tailoring, not as generic cures.
4. Strengthened the distinction between educational accessibility supports and individualized rehabilitation.
5. Preserved medical escalation language for new/worsening neurological danger signs.
